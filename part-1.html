        1
        Introduction
        
      

      
The wristband idea

    


    

      

        
Start here

        
You go to a concert. At the gate, someone checks your ticket and your face. Then they put a paper band on your wrist and wave you through.

        
After that, nobody checks anything again. Not at the food stall. Not at the toilets. Not at the door to the good seats. They just glance at your wrist.

      


      
That little band is doing a surprising amount of work. It says three things at the same time, without anyone having to talk to anyone:


      

        

          It says
          Someone official already checked this person. The hard work happened once, at the gate.
        

        

          It says
          Here is what they may do. A gold band gets into the good seats. A blue one does not.
        

        

          It says
          Faking this is hard. The pattern, the print, the colour; you cannot draw one at home.
        

      


      
A JSON Web Token, JWT, and people say it out loud as "jot", is that wristband, for the internet.


      
It is a small piece of text. A website gives it to you after you prove who you are. From then on, your browser shows it with every request, and the website can read it and trust it without going to look you up anywhere.


      

        The word "claims"
        The handbook calls the facts written on the band claims. A claim is just a statement about someone: this is user 402, her name is Ana, she is an admin. That is all the word means. Don't let it slow you down.
      

    


    
    

      
1.2  What problem does this solve?


      

        
Picture the alternative

        
Same concert, but no wristbands. Now every stall, every door, every guard has to phone the main office and ask: "Is this person allowed in?"

        
One office. One list. Thousands of calls a minute. The line is always busy, and when the office goes down for five minutes, the whole festival stops moving.

      


      
Websites have exactly this problem, and for a long time they solved it exactly this way.


      
The old way is called a session. When you log in, the website writes your details into its own memory and hands you a small ID number. Every click you make, the website takes that number and goes to look you up.


      
It works. But the website has to keep that memory, keep it fast, and share it with every other copy of itself. That memory is the busy phone line.


      
A JWT moves the answer onto you. Instead of a number that means nothing on its own, you carry the actual facts, sealed so you cannot change them. Now every server can decide by itself, in a fraction of a second, with nothing to look up.


      
That is the whole trade. You will see the cost of it later (there is one) but this is the reason JWTs exist.

1.3  So what does one actually look like?

      
Like this. It is one long line of text; the breaks below are only so it fits on the screen.

    


    

      

        

          The three parts
          two dots, three pieces
        

        

      


      

        
Two dots. Three pieces. That is the entire shape of a JWT, and it never changes:

        

          

            Piece 1 · header
            The label. How was this sealed? Not about you at all; it is instructions for the person opening it.
          

          

            Piece 2 · payload
            The message. Who you are, what you may do, how long this lasts. The interesting part.
          

          

            Piece 3 · signature
            The seal. Proof that nobody changed pieces 1 and 2 since they were written.
          

        


        

          The thing almost everyone gets wrong
          It looks like a secret code. It is not. That jumble of letters is not hiding anything; anyone holding the token can read every word inside it, in about one second, with no password. Think of a postcard with a wax seal: the postman can read the message, but he cannot change it without breaking the seal. A JWT protects against editing, not against reading. So never put anything private in one.
        

      

    


    
    

      
1.4  Where it came from

      
Short version, because it matters less than people think.

      
Before 2011, every company invented its own way of doing this, and none of them worked with anyone else's. In 2011 a group of people sat down to agree on one shape. They called themselves JOSE, JSON Object Signing and Encryption. Their drafts became the official documents around 2015.

      
That agreement is the reason a token made by Google can be read by a small app in another country that has never heard of Google. Everybody learned the same handwriting.

      
The main authors were Mike Jones, Nat Sakimura, John Bradley and Joe Hildebrand. The group is quiet now, the work is done.

    


  










  


    

      

        2
        Practical applications
        
      

      
Where you have already met one

      
Before taking the token apart, it helps to see it doing a job. Three jobs, actually, and three ways people break it.

    


    
    

      
2.1  Carrying your own session

      
You met this idea above with the coats. Now the real version.

      
A shop wants to remember what is in your basket. The old way: keep the basket on the server, give you a number. The token way: put the basket itself in the token and let your browser hold it.

      
Here is a real shopping basket, straight from the handbook, before it is folded into a token:

    


    

      

        

          
What it says

          


{
  "items": [ 0, 2, 4 ],
  "iat": 1493139659,   // made at this moment
  "exp": 1493143259    // dead one hour later
}

          

        

        

          
What gets stored in your browser

The shop's server keeps nothing. When you ask for a page, it reads your basket out of the token you sent, checks the seal, and answers. When you add an item, it makes a fresh token with the new list and hands it back.


        

          A detail worth noticing
          The page in your browser reads the basket without checking the seal; it only needs the item names to draw the list, and it cannot keep a secret anyway. The server checks the seal properly, every single time. Front end reads; back end verifies. Never the other way around.
        


        
So is this actually a good idea?

        
Honestly: sometimes. The handbook is blunt about it, and so should we be.

        

          

            Good when
            The thing you are carrying is small. Your servers are many. You want any one of them to answer without asking anyone.
          

          

            Bad when
            The thing is big. A fat token rides along with every single request, forever. You can easily spend more on bandwidth than you saved on lookups.
          

        

        
There is no universal answer. Measure your own traffic. A 4 KB token on a mobile connection, sent a hundred times a minute, is a real cost.

      

    


    
    

      
2.1.1  Three ways people break this

      
Client-side data is data you handed to a stranger and asked them to bring back unchanged. Treat it that way.

    


    
    

      

        

          
Attack one · cutting off the signature

          
Imagine a note from your teacher, signed at the bottom. You want to change what it says, but you cannot copy that signature.

          
So you don't. You cut the signature off entirely, and write at the top: "this one doesn't need a signature."

          
If the person reading it shrugs and accepts that; you have won. That is the whole attack.

Attack two · tricking your browser (CSRF)

          
Your browser is extremely helpful, and that is the problem.

          
Once you are logged into your bank, your browser attaches your bank cookie to every request going to that bank. It never stops to ask who asked for the request. It just helps.

          
So a bad page puts an invisible picture on it. The picture's address is not a picture at all; it is your bank's "send money" page. Your browser goes to fetch the picture, helpfully brings your cookie, and the bank sees a perfectly normal, perfectly signed-in request.

            Why the fix works
            The browser only attaches cookies automatically. A token sent in a header has to be put there by your own JavaScript, and a stranger's page cannot run your JavaScript. Short lives on tokens help too, and so do the usual anti-forgery tricks. But if the token is not a cookie, this particular attack simply has nothing to work with.
          

        

      

    


    
    

      

        

          
Attack three · getting the site to work for you (XSS)

          
Picture a restaurant where the waiter reads out loud whatever a customer writes on a card, and does whatever it says.

          
Most people write happy birthday. Then one person writes: "Waiter, hand over the contents of the till." And the waiter, who only knows how to read cards and do what they say, does it.

          
That is cross-site scripting. Someone leaves a comment that is not really a comment. The site prints it onto everyone else's screen, and the browser (which cannot tell the difference) runs it.

The angle brackets get turned into harmless letters. The browser sees text, draws text, and moves on. The comment shows up looking exactly as it was typed, which is the honest outcome.

            

          

          

            Why this one matters most
            If a stranger can run code inside your page, they can read your cookies, read your local storage, and take your token. No signature stops that, the token they stole is completely genuine. Clean everything that comes from a user. Setting HttpOnly on a cookie hides it from JavaScript and helps a lot, though it does nothing at all for the forgery attack above. Different locks, different doors.
          

        

      

    



    
    

      
2.2  "Continue with Google"

      

        
You have done this a hundred times

        
You open a new app. It asks you to make an account. You do not want another password, so you tap Continue with Google, a familiar screen appears, you tap once more, and you are in.

        
Think of a passport. One office issues it. Then dozens of countries that have never spoken to you accept it, because they trust that office. You do not apply for a fresh passport at every border.

      

      
The proper name for this is federated identity. One place holds who you are; everybody else agrees to trust that place. What travels between them, very often, is a JWT.

2.2.1  Two tokens, two jobs

      

        
The hotel

        
You check in at reception and show your passport. They give you a key card. The card opens your room, works until Sunday, and nothing else.

        
If you drop that card in the street, someone has a key, but only to one room, and only until Sunday. Meanwhile your passport never left your pocket. That is the point of the arrangement.

      

      
Logins work the same way, with two different tokens:

      

        

          Access token  · the key card
          Short life, minutes. Sent with every request. Because it is signed, the door can check it on its own, without phoning reception. That is the speed.
        

        

          Refresh token  · the right to a new card
          Long life, days or weeks. Used rarely, and only against reception. Guard this one. If it leaks, someone can keep making new key cards.
        

      

      
Watch the two of them working together. The clock below runs fast so you can see a day pass in a few seconds.

Where OAuth2 and OpenID Connect fit

      
Two names you will keep bumping into. They are not competitors to JWT; they sit on top of it.

      
OAuth2 is the rulebook for the dance you just walked through. Oddly, it never says what a token should look like inside. A signed JWT happens to fit its needs perfectly, so most people use one.

      
OpenID Connect fills in the parts OAuth2 left open, and it does say what the token looks like: a JWT. It adds one called the ID token, whose only job is to tell the app who you are (your name, your picture, your email) so it can say "Hi Ana" without asking anyone.

      
Refresh tokens usually are not JWTs, by the way. They always require a trip to the server anyway, so a plain random string does the job.

Opening it up

      
Now the slow look. What each piece contains, why it is written the way it is, and how to build one by hand.

    


    
    

      
3.0  First, the thing that confuses everybody

      

        
Base64 is not a secret code

        
You are on a bad phone line, spelling your name. "S for sugar, W for water, A for apple…"

        
You are not hiding your name. Anyone listening understands you perfectly. You are just using sounds that survive a bad connection.

      

      
That is Base64. The internet is a bad phone line for certain characters, spaces, quote marks, curly brackets, accented letters. They get mangled inside web addresses and headers.

      
So Base64 rewrites anything at all using only 64 characters that always survive: A–Z, a–z, 0–9, and two more. JWTs use a version called base64url, which swaps those last two for - and _ so the result is safe to paste into a link.

      
Anyone can undo it. That is the point; it is a transport wrapper, not a lock. Try it both ways:

    


    

      

        

          Try it · base64url, both directions
          Type in either box.
        

        

          

            
            
encode →← decode

            
          

          

            
Three bytes in, four characters out

            

          

          

            Base64 works in blocks: it takes three bytes, treats them as 24 bits, and cuts them into four groups of six. Six bits has 64 possible values, hence the name. The output is always about a third longer than what went in.
          

        

      

    


    
    

      
3.1  The header

      
The smallest piece, and the least interesting, which is exactly why it gets skipped and then causes trouble. It is the label on the envelope. It says nothing about you; it tells the reader how to handle what is inside.

      

        
        
        
      

      
An unsealed header is as small as JSON gets:

          Why the names are so short
          Not to look clever. This whole thing gets sent with every single request. Writing exp instead of expiration_time saves eleven characters, times a few hundred million requests a day, that is real bandwidth and real money. The three-letter names are a budget decision.
        

        
All the times are counted in seconds since 1 January 1970, which is how computers have kept time for decades. 1789084740 is a real date; it just does not look like one.

      

    


    
    

      
3.2.2  Your own fields

      
The seven above are the ones the standard defines. Everything else is yours to invent, and that is where two systems can quietly disagree.

      
Say you add a field called role. Somewhere else, another company also adds role, meaning something completely different. The day your two systems meet, one of them reads the other's token and confidently gets it wrong.

      

        

          Private claim
          Just for you. role, plan, cart_id. Fine when only your own code will ever read the token.
        

        

          Public claim
          Safe to share. Either registered in the official list, or given a name nobody else could collide with, usually by putting your web address in front: https://myapp.com/role.
        

      

      
One more small rule: never write the same field twice in one payload. Some readers keep the last one, some throw the whole token out. Neither is what you wanted.

    


    
    

      
3.3  Tokens with no seal at all

      
You can leave the seal off. Set alg to "none", write your payload, and stop. The result has only two pieces, but it still ends with a dot, because the third piece exists and is simply empty.

      
When is that reasonable? When nothing bad happens if the holder edits it. The handbook's example: an app remembering your display name and which page you were last on, so it can draw the screen before the real data arrives. If someone changes their own display name to Batman, they have gained nothing but a slightly odd screen.

      
In real life these are rare. If a decision depends on it, it needs a seal.

3.5  Reading one back

      
Going the other way is even simpler, and you can do it in your head if you had a base64 table handy:

      
Find the first dot. Take everything before it, undo the base64; that is the header. Take everything between the first and second dot, undo the base64; that is the payload. Done.

      

        Reading is not checking
        Those steps needed no secret and no permission, which is worth sitting with for a moment. Anyone who gets hold of your token can read every field in it. Checking the seal is a completely separate act, it needs the secret, and only the server can do it.

How the seal actually works

      
Chapters 1 to 3 keep pointing at the signature without opening it. Since you have already used it three times on this page, here it is, the short, honest version.

      

        
Two friends and a secret word

        
You and a friend agree on a secret word. Nobody else knows it.

        
To send a message, you mix the message together with the secret word using a fixed recipe, and you get a jumble of characters. You send the message and the jumble.

        
Your friend does exactly the same mixing with the same secret word. If she gets the same jumble, the message arrived exactly as you wrote it. If a single letter changed on the way, her jumble comes out completely different, and she knows.

      

      
That is HS256. The recipe is called HMAC-SHA256, the "secret word" is a string on your server, and the jumble is the third piece of the token.

      
The exact thing that gets mixed is worth memorising, because it explains a lot:

      
signature = HMAC_SHA256( base64url(header) + "." + base64url(payload), secret )

      
The header and payload are inside the mixture. Change one character of either, and the seal no longer matches. That is why editing a token is pointless without the secret, and why the secret must never, ever reach the browser.

      
Below, this page really does the mixing, in your browser, with the real algorithm. Break something and watch it fail.

        Putting it together
        
      

      
What a careful server actually does

      
Every idea on this page (the seal, the clock, the audience, the three attacks) meets here, in the six checks a token has to survive before a single word of it is believed.

    


    

      

        
Six gates, not one

        
People imagine checking a token is one action: is the signature good?

        
It is six, in order, and the signature is only the fourth. A token can be perfectly sealed and still be thrown out by the two gates after it, and it can be thrown out by the two gates before it without the seal ever being looked at.

          Pick a token above. It will travel down the six gates and either come out the bottom, or be pushed out to the right at the first gate it fails.
        

      

    


    

      

        

          The two before the signature
          Gates 2 and 3 are the ones people skip, and they are where alg:none and the public-key-as-secret trick get in. Both are decided by your configuration, not by anything in the token.
        

        

          The two after it
          Gates 5 and 6 catch tokens that are completely genuine, just old, or meant for somebody else. No amount of cryptography helps here; only checking the claims does.
        

      

      

        Read the order out loud once
        Structure, algorithm, key, signature, clock, audience. If your validation code does not have all six, the missing one is where you will be attacked, and the two most often missing are the last two, because they feel like paperwork rather than security.
