        4
        JSON Web Signatures
        
      

      
Who is allowed to make one

    


    

      
In part one you saw a seal being made and broken. What you did not see is the question that decides how a whole system is built: if I let you check my seals, have I also let you make them?

      
With the method used so far, HS256, the answer is yes. And that turns out to matter enormously.


      

        
Two ways to prove something is yours

        
A rubber stamp. Your head office has one. A branch office needs to check that stamped letters are genuine, so you post them a copy of the stamp. Now that branch can check letters, and stamp new ones. You cannot give away one ability without the other.

        
A handwritten signature. You sign a cheque. The bank keeps a sample of your signature on file and compares. They can check your signature for the rest of your life. They can never write it.

      


      
That is the entire difference between the two families of JWT signature, and it is worth saying slowly:


      

        

          HS256 · the rubber stamp
          One shared secret. Anyone who can check a token can also make one. Fine when the same people are on both ends.
        

        

          RS256 / ES256 · the signature
          A pair of keys. The private one signs. The public one only checks. You can hand the public one to the whole world.
        

      


      
The handbook calls these shared secret and public key schemes. Watch what happens when you have more than two parties.

    


    

      

        

          Try it · one head office, twelve branches
          All twelve need to check tokens. What do you post them?
        

        

          

            Your head office issues the tokens. Twelve branches have to check them, so you must send
            each branch something. Press each option and watch what those twelve
            branches can then do.
          

          

            
            
          

          

          

        

      

    


    

      
The handbook puts it plainly: with a shared secret, "if a legitimate user turned malicious, he or she could modify messages without the other parties noticing." With a key pair, that user only ever held the public key, and the public key cannot write anything.

      
This is why every large login provider, the ones whose tokens are checked by thousands of apps they have never met, signs with RS256 or ES256 and publishes the public key for anyone to fetch.

      

        Three names, one idea
        HS256 is HMAC with SHA-256, the shared secret. RS256 is RSA, the classic key pair. ES256 is the same idea on elliptic curves: different mathematics, much smaller keys, same guarantee. Only HS256 is compulsory for a library to support; the other two are recommended. Everything else you will see (HS384, RS512, PS256…) is a variation on those three.

4.1  "Valid" means more than "correctly sealed"

      

        
A perfect ticket for yesterday

        
You hand the gate a ticket. The hologram is flawless. It is completely genuine.

        
It is also dated yesterday, and it is for a different line.

        
The gate has two separate jobs, and doing the first one brilliantly does not excuse skipping the second.

      

      
The handbook is strict about this word. Validating a token does not mean checking the seal. It means checking everything the header and the payload ask for, and everything you require on top.

      

        
Check the sealIs it really from who it claims? And was the algorithm one I allow?

        
Check the clockexp passed? nbf not reached yet? Then it does not matter how perfect the seal is.

        
Check it is for youIs my name in aud? Is iss someone I actually trust?

      

      
Two consequences follow, and both surprise people. A token with "alg": "none" can technically be "valid", the header asked for nothing, so nothing failed. And a token with a flawless signature can be entirely invalid, because it expired an hour ago.

    


    
    

      
4.2  Extra lines in the header

      
Once you move to key pairs, a new problem appears: which key? A server might be holding five public keys. The token has to say which one it was signed with. That is what these header fields are for.

      

        
        
        
        
        
      


      

        The trap in jwk
        A token that carries its own key is a note that says "here is my signature sample, compare it to the one I have also enclosed." Of course it matches; the forger wrote both. It is the same family as the stripping trick from part one, and the fix is the same shape: only ever check against a key you already trust, one you fetched yourself or were configured with. Never one the token handed you.
      


      
Here is why kid earns its place. Keys have to be changed sometimes, and changing a key is exactly like changing the locks on a building full of people who are still inside.

4.3  When one signature is not enough

      

        
A contract, not a ticket

        
A ticket stub has room for exactly one stamp. That is fine; one railway issued it.

        
A contract is different. The same document, signed at the bottom by three different people, each with their own pen.

      

      
The dotted form you have been reading all along is called compact serialization, and it holds exactly one signature. When you need more, JWS offers a second shape: the whole thing written out as ordinary JSON, with a list of signatures.

      
The example below is straight from the specification. One payload, signed twice (once with RSA, once with elliptic curve) so that readers who can only check one kind still have something they can check.

    


    

      

        

          Three shapes, one token
          The payload never changes. Only the way it is written down does.
        

        

          

            A signed token can be written in three different shapes. Press one to see
            the same token in that shape, and what it buys you.
          

          

            
            
            
          

          

          

        

      


      

        
Two kinds of header, and the difference matters

        
The JSON shape introduces something the compact form does not have: a header that is not covered by the signature.

        

          

            protected
            Written on the document itself. The signature covers it. Change one letter and the signature breaks.
          

          

            header  (unprotected)
            A sticky note attached to it. Anyone can peel it off and write a new one. Useful for hints, never for decisions.
          

        

        
At least one of the two must be there; both may be. The real header is the two of them added together, and no field may appear twice. In practice kid often lives on the sticky note; it is only a hint about which key to try, and trying the wrong key simply fails the check anyway.

      

    


  










  


    

      

        5
        JSON Web Encryption
        
      

      
When it must not be read

      
Everything so far has been a postcard. Readable by anyone, protected only from editing. Now we put it in an envelope.

    


    

      
The moment you encrypt a token, one thing reverses, and it catches almost everybody, because it feels backwards the first time you meet it.


      

        
The notice board and the suggestion box

        
Signing is a notice board. One person pins up notices. Everyone in the building can read them and check the handwriting is really the manager's. Nobody else can pin a genuine notice. Information flows from one writer to many readers.

        
Encrypting is a suggestion box. It has a slot in the top. Anyone walking past can drop a note in. Only the person with the box key can open it and read. Information flows from many writers to one reader.

      


      
Same pair of keys, opposite directions. Written out, it is almost startling:


      

        

          
            
Signing  (JWS)Encrypting  (JWE)

          
          
            
Who holds the private keythe writer, signsthe reader, decrypts

            
Who holds the public keythe readers, check onlythe writers, encrypt only

            
So data flowsone → manymany → one

          
        

      


      

        The consequence people miss
        Encryption does not replace signing. If anyone can drop a note in your suggestion box, then opening a note tells you what it says, not who wrote it. A liar can post one too. That is why the handbook says the two are complementary, and why serious systems do both: sign it so the reader knows who wrote it, then encrypt it so only the reader can read it.
      


      
And no, you cannot fix this by handing the private key to every writer. That just turns it back into a shared secret, and you have thrown away the only thing key pairs were good for.

    


    
    

      
5.1  Five parts now, not three

      
An encrypted token has a different shape. Four dots, five pieces. Before the list, the reason, because the reason is the whole design.


      

        
Posting a locked trunk

        
You want to send a heavy trunk to a friend, and nobody along the way may look inside.

        
You own a clever little lock that only your friend can open. But it is tiny and painfully slow to work, and it will not close a trunk.

        
So you do it in two steps. Lock the trunk with an ordinary strong padlock, fast, and it handles any size. Then drop that padlock's key into the tiny clever lock-box that only your friend can open, and tape the box to the trunk.

        
Your friend opens the little box, takes out the key, opens the trunk.

      


      
That is exactly what an encrypted JWT does, and it is why there are five pieces instead of three.


      

        

          The padlock · enc
          Fast, handles any amount of data. A brand-new random key is made for this one message. The handbook calls it the Content Encryption Key, or CEK. It locks the actual message.
        

        

          The little lock-box · alg
          Slow, tiny, and only your reader can open it. Its only job is to carry that one small key safely. This is piece two of the token.
        

      


      

        Why the header has two algorithms
        This trips everyone up on first reading. alg is the little lock-box, how the key was wrapped. enc is the padlock, how the message itself was locked. Two algorithms, because there are genuinely two locks.
      


      
With that in mind, the five pieces read themselves:

    


    

      

        
        
        
        
        
      


      

        

          Why the tag has to be there
          Without it, an attacker who cannot read your message can still scribble on it, flip a few characters of the locked text and post it on. The reader unlocks it, gets nonsense, and processes the nonsense. Sometimes the way it fails leaks information, one small piece at a time. The tag stops that at the door: if a single bit changed, unlocking refuses to even begin. This is why the spec insists on it.

5.2  Five ways to get the key across

      
The specification lists five ways of settling on that padlock key. They sound like five different worlds; they are really one question, what, if anything, goes in piece two?

    


    

      

        

          Key Encryption
          The little lock-box, using a key pair. The one in the workshop above, and the one you will meet most.
          piece 2 filled
        

        

          Key Wrapping
          Same idea, but the box is locked with a secret you already share, rather than a key pair.
          piece 2 filled
        

        

          Key Agreement with Wrapping
          Both sides work out a shared key from their own key pairs, then use it to wrap the padlock key.
          piece 2 filled
        

        

          Direct Key Agreement
          The clever one. Both sides independently arrive at the same key without it ever being sent. Nothing to wrap.
          piece 2 empty
        

        

          Direct Encryption
          You already share a secret key, so use it as the padlock key itself. Nothing to wrap.
          piece 2 empty
        

      


      

        
So when you meet a token with two dots in a row and nothing between them, that is not a bug. It is one of the bottom two modes, saying there was nothing to send.

        

          One more distinction worth keeping straight
          The CEK is the padlock key, the thing that actually locks your message. The JWE Encrypted Key is the wrapped-up version of it that travels in piece two. Same key, two states: in your hand, or in the box.
        

      

    


    

      
Several readers, one trunk

      
JWE has a written-out JSON shape too, and it does something rather elegant. Encrypting for three people does not mean three encrypted copies of your message.

      
You lock the trunk once. Then you tape three little lock-boxes to it; one for each friend, each holding the same padlock key. Everyone opens their own box, gets the same key, opens the same trunk.

      
In the JSON form that is a recipients list: one shared ciphertext, and one encrypted_key per person.

    


  










  

    

      

        ✦
        Putting both together
        
      

      
Sign it, then hide it

    


    

      
You now have two tools that do different jobs. A signature says who wrote this. Encryption says only you may read it. When you need both answers, you use both; one inside the other. The handbook calls this a nested JWT.

      
The order is not a matter of taste.

    


    

      

      

        
Why signing has to come first

        
Sign then encrypt, and the signature ends up inside the envelope. An attacker cannot cut it off, because they cannot get in there at all, and the whole stripping trick from part one dies quietly.

        
Do it the other way round and the signature sits on the outside of a box nobody can inspect. Anyone can peel it off and stick their own on.

        
The handbook adds a careful footnote: this order only works because JWE always carries that authentication tag, so the locked message is already protected from tampering. For encryption schemes without one, the usual advice runs the other way.

        

          And when you do not need both
          If the same system writes and reads the token, a shared-secret encrypted token already tells you both things; nobody else could have made it. Nesting matters when the writer and the reader are genuinely different parties.
